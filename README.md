## The renewal price decides the two-year cost

Two examples from the budget end show how far sticker price and total cost drift apart. AccuWeb's $2/mo WordPress plan requires a three-year contract, so you pay $72 up front and renew at $3/mo from year four. Hostinger's $3/mo rate requires a four-year commitment, $144 up front, after which the plan renews at $11/mo.

RackNerd's 30 GB plan at $5.59/mo costs $134.16 over 24 months at the listed rate, with no teaser period to expire.

That comparison cuts both ways. Over three years, AccuWeb's $72 total beats RackNerd's $201.24 on price alone. What the higher monthly rate buys is a shorter commitment if you pick one in the cart, plus cPanel and free migration included on the cheapest tier. The rule that keeps you out of trouble: multiply the intro rate by the number of months you're locked into, add the renewal rate for the months after, and compare that number across providers. A $2.99 headline is only cheaper than $5.59 if you stay long enough to amortize the prepayment and don't move the site.

How long will this site exist, and how likely are you to move it? Under 18 months, or a project that might change direction, and a flat rate wins. Committed for three years or more with price as the only criterion, and a prepaid long-term contract wins.

## When shared hosting is the wrong purchase

The hosts themselves define this tier as low-resource. GoDaddy scopes shared hosting to blogs, informational sites and smaller stores. OVHcloud sends high-traffic stores and CMS installations with many extensions to a performance plan or a VPS. Hostinger sells VPS with full root access for users who need dedicated resources.

Three signals that you've outgrown it: traffic that keeps tripping resource limits, a plugin stack heavy enough that page loads drag under shared CPU, or a need for root access and custom server software. RackNerd sells VPS, reseller and dedicated plans alongside shared hosting if you get there.
